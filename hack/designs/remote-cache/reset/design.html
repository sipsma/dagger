1. The goal

A run on engine A executes a build graph; its cache results (metadata + content) become available to engine B, which adopts equivalent results as cache hits and materializes their content on demand — downloading snapshot content when available, re-executing the recipe when not. The near-term proof is unchanged: a cold CI run seeds the cache; a warm run on fresh engines shows a real, expected-scale improvement, with counters that demonstrate actual adoption and reuse — and that would have failed loudly if reuse hadn't happened.

The first concrete configuration of that goal is the restart case: one engine persists to disk, a fresh engine boots on that state and gets warm hits. It is the same problem as the remote case with the transport removed, and it is provable entirely in-repo (R11).

Scope of this take (owner-confirmed): engine changes only. The remote service/transport is not part of this batch of work — it stays in the goal statement above as the end state everything must remain compatible with, but nothing here designs or builds it.

2. The load-bearing structural decision

Two layers stay separate, permanently. The identity layer — the e-graph: equivalence classes over calls and results, merged live as work completes — stays exactly as it is on main. Content state (filesystem snapshots, metadata, materialization status) has exactly one home per result, mutated only at well-defined decision points under ordinary locking. Interchangeability of equivalents is a decision-point policy: consulted at lookup (pick one winner and materialize that result), at publication, and at import — never enforced continuously across shared mutable state. Runs build private values, as they do on main today, and touch the cache only when they publish.

The tempting alternative is named here so it stays dead: sharing content state across equivalence classes and reconciling it continuously, so any equivalent result can serve a sibling's content at any moment, mid-run included. That coupling looks attractive whenever dedup or "why not just share it" comes up — and it drags in run-scoped isolation machinery (overlays, reader-audience classification, evaluation gates, multi-lock protocols) whose correctness surface is every reader, writer, and entry point in the engine. It is forbidden by R5, R15, and the non-goals; a proposal heading there is R15's tripwire: stop and escalate before building.

3. Requirements and non-goals

Normative text. Adjudication history and provenance live in the review log (§4).

R1 — Dual materialization paths

A result must be re-creatable on an engine that didn't produce it, by content transfer or by recomputation — at least one retained path, both when possible (some results have no in-engine recomputation path — e.g. client uploads, whose recipe lives on the client machine; their retained path is their content). Materializing a result must not destroy its recipe; persisting a result must be able to carry value, recipe, or both.

R2 — No stranded hits

Adopting a result as a cache hit is a promise: the result will materialize on demand via some retained source, or the adoption itself was a bug. Preferred-source failure falls through to the next source. Silent wrong answers and use-time "missing snapshot" errors are design failures.

R3 — Portable semantic identity

Every identity that crosses an engine boundary (persistence file, export bundle, backend row) is either a semantic digest or a durable origin identity. Engine-local allocator IDs never serialize. Importing the same result twice is idempotent.

R4 — Content-addressed snapshot identity, lazy at export

Immutable snapshot content crossing any boundary is identified by its content (layer diffID chains), never by local snapshotter keys. Mutable-owner snapshots never cross boundaries as content. Hard constraint: content identity is computed lazily at export time (hash-while-packing), never eagerly during normal execution — eager content-hashing is a previously-hit, real performance trap.

R5 — Materialization state: one home per result, surgery-proof

Every result's materialization state (materialized / remotely materializable / recomputable, plus the content itself once present) has exactly one home, owned by the cache, keyed by the result. It is written only at well-defined points — publication of locally-completed work, lazy content arrival for that result, import — under the cache's ordinary locking. Clones, copies, detached views, and mount-replay reference the home — so a copy can never become the only holder of, nor lose its path to, the result's content or the way to re-make it — and they never copy its state or acquire homes of their own. Content state is never shared across results: if two equivalent results both have content, that is tolerated redundancy for GC, not a consistency problem to reconcile.

R6 — Explicit execution identity

Deferred/recomputed work runs under an explicit principal (the forcing waiter's), with retry on principal death; the identity of a result is fully determined by its recipe and never contaminated by whoever forced materialization.

R7 — Session-resource soundness extends to foreign results

An adopted result that transitively requires secret/socket handles is only adoptable by sessions holding those handles — same rule as today, surviving import. (The known per-engine secret-salt issue remains deliberately deferred and separately tracked; it partitions caches across engines until addressed.)

R8 — Falsifiable observability

Every lookup outcome, adoption decision, materialization-source execution, and fallback is observable with structured vocabulary; every run can produce a reuse summary; proofs and tests can turn unexpected outcomes into hard failures. Landings are validated against pre-registered, falsifiable predictions (state what the evidence must show before running the test), and forensic diagnostics that earn their keep at a failure site are made permanent rather than left as temporary instruments.

R9 — One portable result encoding

Local persistence and remote export share one encoding for results (frames, payload envelopes, snapshot links, recipes). The surrounding structure differs by design: local persistence may additionally dump engine-local index rows for fast same-engine restart — subject to R16 — while bundles carry none of that. No second, looser result-serialization model.

R10 — Identity-stable metadata

Import → re-export cycles add zero result rows. Genuinely new executions add honestly-new rows; independently-produced equivalents remain distinct rows related by semantic digests, and collapsing those for serving is backend merge policy on the digest layer — the engine's obligations are stable row identity, faithful digest emission, and a self-check metric.

R11 — In-repo provability, gated continuously

The full adoption/hydration/fallback model is exercisable by integration tests inside dagger/dagger with no cloud stack. A curated integration set is a standing gate for every landed chunk of work, not a milestone-end event: anchors are the persistence/restart suite, the cross-session-caching tests, and a selection of the services tests; agents may add tests they judge valuable, within reason. Budget is deliberately not encoded as a number here — the full gate on the order of a quarter-hour is acceptable, focused subsets are for iteration, and the owner monitors actual costs. Legitimate intermediate failures are named, temporary, owner-acknowledged exceptions.

R12 — Sparse, demand-bounded materialization

Materializing a result moves and creates only what is strictly required: the content path touches exactly the needed chain blobs; the recipe path recurses only into the inputs the recipe actually reads, itself demand-driven; adoption, viability, and import are metadata-only and move zero content. The canonical scenario: a huge tangled DAG culminating in one result — a warm run that reads one file from it hydrates that file's chain and nothing else.

Granularity clarification (owner-ruled). The load-bearing floor is: materializing a result must never require materializing its dependency DAG. Granularity within a single result is deliberately open: for a multi-output result (e.g. an exec's filesystem + its stdout/exit metadata), materializing outputs individually and materializing them together are both acceptable — choose whichever is simpler and more cohesive. The recipe path already materializes all of a result's outputs together, so insisting on per-output granularity for the content path creates an asymmetry between the two paths — don't pay complexity for that without a strong, stated reason. A design-time consideration (not a requirement): very small outputs (exit codes, small streams) may simply live in metadata rather than participate in content materialization at all.

R13 — Recipes are closed over engine-ambient state

Any engine-local input that shapes a result — native platform, the engine secret salt, engine/schema version, anything read off server state during planning or resolution — must either be canonicalized into the recipe (so equivalent work matches and different work partitions) or explicitly partition the cache. Identity that silently depends on which engine ran the call fails silently: wrong adoptions or permanent misses.

R14 — Startup-only import; the serving-time cache is read-mostly

All bulk ingestion of identity and result metadata — disk restore and remote bundle import — completes before the engine serves sessions, under coarse locking (these are fast, batched metadata writes; there is no pause-time problem to engineer around). While serving, cache state changes only through publication of locally-completed work and lazy content arrival on a result's own home (R5). Continuous / mid-serving import is an explicit non-goal for this take; if a future take adds it, it arrives with its own explicit quiesce design and may not weaken R15 to do so.

R15 — No new cross-cutting concurrency invariants

The design builds on the engine's existing locking and singleflight structure, and this document is the source of truth for its concurrency model. The strong default: no new cross-cutting concurrency invariants — reader-audience classifications, run-scoped transactions or overlays over shared state, multi-lock protocols spanning packages. Working test: if a proposed mechanism requires auditing "every reader / every writer / every entry point" to be correct, treat that as a tripwire — stop, escalate, and discuss before building.

Two clarifications so this constraint cuts where intended. First, it does not forbid ordinary, contained engineering: adding a mutex around a data structure, a singleflight on a call path, or similar locally-reasoned synchronization is normal work, not an exception to this requirement. Second, this is a design constraint, not a taboo: if a cross-cutting mechanism ever appears genuinely necessary, the path is to make the case, get explicit agreement, and write it into this design before implementation — necessity and agreement are both required, and any such proposal should be treated with the utmost suspicion precisely because this class of mechanism is where the previous attempt's complexity came from. Interchangeability of equivalents, specifically, is a decision-point policy (pick a winner at lookup), never continuous reconciliation of shared mutable state.

R16 — Restart-deterministic lookup identity

Warm lookup keys are a pure function of persisted, content-addressed identity — a restarted (or foreign) engine derives identical keys for identical calls regardless of in-memory assignment order, timing, or concurrent load. Any key component derived from memory-assigned ordinals (class numbers, allocator IDs, insertion order) is either recomputed canonically from persisted digests on load or never participates in persisted identity at all. This is cheaply testable as a property (boot a fresh engine on persisted state, derive keys, compare) — a determinism test, not an integration saga.

Clarification — what "identical calls" means here. The engine deliberately supports identity scoped narrower than content: per-client, per-session, and per-call inputs exist on purpose, make two calls genuinely non-identical across engines (or across sessions), and are fully legal under this requirement — they simply never produce cross-engine key matches themselves, and results downstream of them that acquire content-addressed identity are where sharing resumes, by design. R16 constrains only accidental divergence: bookkeeping artifacts leaking into the keys of calls that are semantically identical. Deliberate cache partitioning is a feature; timing-dependent cache partitioning is the bug.

Corollary — Partial local persistence

A deliberate local corollary of R1+R2+R5: local persistent cache stops being all-or-nothing. Today any inconsistency wipes the whole store; with per-result source state and adoption gating, a result whose snapshot was lost falls back to its recipe or is dropped (with its dependents), keeping the rest. A user-visible robustness win with zero remote-cache dependency — in scope (owner-confirmed); sequencing within the take is decided in Part II.

Explicit non-goals (this take) NON-GOALS

  • Live / continuous import while serving. Superseded by R14. Import happens at startup; later arrivals wait for the next boot (or a future, separately-designed quiesce).
  • Content interchangeability as a continuous runtime invariant. Superseded by R15 + R5. Equivalents are interchangeable at lookup time; nothing moves content between results after the fact.
  • Cross-result / cross-class sharing of content state (shared records, fill-if-absent reconciliation, live healing). Redundant content between equivalent results is acceptable and garbage-collectible.
  • Run-scoped transactional isolation over cache state (overlays, forks, commit/discard, reader-audience splits). With R5+R15 there is no mid-run shared state to isolate; runs build private values and publish once.
  • The remote service/transport itself. Out of this take entirely (§1); the engine work must only remain compatible with it.

6. Target shape (ratified round 4 — living, adjustable)

The one-screen picture of the system this design builds, stated mechanism-neutrally — every "how" here is owned by a later section, and two explicitly open questions are flagged rather than implied away.

The shape, in prose

  • Boot. The engine restores its local persisted state and imports any provided bundles before serving sessions, under coarse locking (R14). The identity index is rebuilt such that lookup keys are pure functions of persisted digests (R16) — no dependence on reconstruction order or timing.
  • Serving — lookups. A lookup consults the identity layer (the e-graph, unchanged from main) and resolves to at most one winning result. A winner whose content is not yet local is still a normal cache hit: its content materializes on demand via a retained source — local snapshot, content transfer (in the remote future), or re-running the value's persisted lazy form — falling through on failure (R1, R2), moving only what is demanded (R12).
  • Serving — publication. Completed local work appends results and teaches equivalence exactly as main does today. Content state is written once, to that result's single home (R5). Runs build private values; the cache never exposes in-progress state because there is none to expose.
  • Shutdown. Persisted state is flushed using the one portable result encoding (R9): per result, value, recipe, or both (R1).

Cross-section invariants

  • One content home per result; references, never copies (R5).
  • The cache mutates at decision points only: boot-import, publication, lazy content arrival, shutdown flush (R14, R15).
  • Lookup-key determinism across restart (R16).
  • No new cross-cutting concurrency machinery anywhere in the above (R15).

Explicitly open questions (owned by later sections, not decided here)

  • Is on-demand materialization of a restored result simply the engine's existing lazy-evaluation path? It might be — restored-result hydration and lazy evaluation have the same shape (a result whose value isn't computed yet, computed on first demand). If so, no new wait/claim machinery exists at all. If not, the content-home section must argue why and design the difference minimally. Deliberately undecided; nothing in this doc may presuppose either answer. → answered by §7 (draft, pending review).
  • Metadata growth under winner-at-lookup. Tolerating redundant content between equivalent results (GC instead of reconciliation) must be shown to satisfy R10 and R15 simultaneously — the content-home section carries an explicit metadata-growth model. → answered by §7 (draft, pending review).

7. The content home: result & materialization state (draft, under discussion)

The idea in three sentences. Every cached result gets exactly one place that answers "what does this result have, and how can it be re-made?" — a small record on the result's sharedResult — the cache's single in-memory entry per result. The values your code touches (Directory, File, Container) stay exactly what they are on main; the LazyAccessor fields they hold content in become caches of that record's answer rather than competing sources of truth. Everything else in this section — the source list, the fall-through order, the growth model — is that one idea meeting the requirements.

Names here are the codebase's own: a value struct is the Go object handlers touch (Directory, File, Container); its accessors are the LazyAccessor fields holding content on that struct (Directory.Snapshot, Container.FS, …) — several per struct; the sharedResult is the cache's single in-memory entry per result (dagql/cache.go). The proposed materializationState lives on the sharedResult — §7 calls it "the home" as shorthand.

Three facts about this arrangement matter for everything below:

Fact 1 — a LazyAccessor is a one-field, compute-once content holder, and clones sometimes copy it. LazyAccessor is get-or-compute-once around a value (core/lazy_state.go:73). When schema handlers build a derived object they clone the parent, and some of those clones copy the accessor (e.g. CloneContainerMetaSnapshot, core/container.go:906). A copy taken before the accessor was filled is a fork: the original may later realize content the copy will never see.

Fact 2 — restored results already come back to life lazily. This is the load-bearing discovery of this section, so here is the actual shape of it. At boot, persisted results are loaded as identity plus a persisted envelope each — the typed payload with its snapshot references, which are local refKeys kept alive by leases (they never cross an engine boundary; R4's local carve-out). Almost no values decode at boot — a small subset eager-decodes immediately, solely to populate the session-resource gating metadata that candidate eligibility needs (cache_persistence_import.go:404); content is never touched. 7.2  The design: one home per result

D1 — The home lives on the sharedResult

Each sharedResult owns a materializationState: the envelope, the ordered sources, and a realized flag. It is written at exactly the four points in Fig. 3 — publication, import, a sharedResult's own lazy realization, and flush (which only reads) — all under the cache's existing locking. No new locks, no state shared between results (R5, R15). Why the sharedResult and not the value: it is the one object with the same lifetime as the result's identity — value structs come and go (decoded, cloned, discarded), so the sharedResult is where a "promise to materialize" (R2) can actually live.

7.3  A warm hit, end to end

The whole model in one story. An engine restarts; a session re-issues the same container().from(base).withExec(…) chain it ran yesterday.

  1. BOOT · import decision point (R14, R16)
    Persisted results are restored: the identity index, and per result a sharedResult carrying {envelope, sources: [local-snapshot, lazy-form], realized: false}. No content touched; no values decode at boot beyond the small subset that populates session-resource gating metadata.
  2. LOOKUP
    The session's call resolves through the e-graph (unchanged from main) to its sharedResult — a hit, one winner, picked once.
  3. FIRST USE · the existing lazy path
    Something actually reads the value. The lazy machinery starts one runner; concurrent callers wait — the same singleflight main runs today (Fact 2).
  4. REALIZE · source ①
    The runner walks the sharedResult's sources: local-snapshot first — refKeys still present, leases acquired → decode the envelope → value struct + accessors filled → the sharedResult marks itself realized (the lazy-arrival write point).
  5. SERVE
    The value flows to the caller; every later use takes the realized fast path.
  6. COUNTERFACTUAL · source ② (fall-through, R2)
    Suppose the snapshot had been pruned: source ① reports not-viable, the runner falls through to the lazy form and re-runs it — the same persisted lazy struct main decodes and runs today (R6 principal semantics) — and the output lands on this sharedResult, which realizes as above.
  7. COUNTERFACTUAL · zero sources (restore section)
    Had no source been viable at boot, the result and its dependents would have been dropped during restore: the call cold-misses and recomputes honestly. A hit is a promise or it isn't made (R2) — and one bad result no longer wipes the store (Fact 3, retired).

8. Persistence: encoding, flush, and restore (draft, under discussion)

The idea in three sentences. What hits disk is each result's materializationState (§7), written in the one portable encoding — and it mostly already does: main persists every result's value (including its lazy form, when it has one) and its snapshot links, so §7's source list needs vetting logic, not new schema. What must change is identity: today's persisted lookup keys hash in-memory integer class IDs, which is exactly the accident R16 forbids — so keys become process-local, recomputed from persisted digests at boot. And restore stops being all-or-nothing: each result is vetted individually, kept if any source is viable, dropped with its dependents if not.

Names here are the codebase's own: the SQLite store lives at dagql/persistdb/schema.sql; a frame (call_frame_json) is a result's persisted call — identity and decode context, never a materialization source; the envelope (self_payload) is the persisted value, carrying its form — snapshot-backed, or lazy (a registered lazy struct referencing other results); eq_class_digests maps equivalence classes to content digests; terms/term_inputs are the structural lookup index; result_snapshot_links are a result's local snapshot refKeys.

  1. R16's implementation: lookup keys become process-local — recomputed from persisted digests at boot. The persisted term_digest column is removed (schema_version bump). Integer IDs stay in the file as ordinary foreign keys, never as identity.
  2. Restore becomes per-result vetting with drop-with-dependents; the wholesale wipe survives only for schema_mismatch and unclean_shutdown. This is where the partial-persistence corollary lands.
  3. Flush stops walking value structs: each result serializes from its materializationState (§7) instead.

8.2  Design

D1 — What flush writes, and where it gets it

Per result, flush writes exactly what today's tables already hold:

  • the value → the envelope, into results.self_payload;
  • the local-snapshot source → its refKeys, into result_snapshot_links;
  • the lazy-form source → nothing new at all: it is already inside the envelope (LazyKind/LazyJSON, referencing the results in result_deps).

What changes is where the encoder gets the snapshot refs. Today it walks the value structs and asks them what refs they hold (Fact 3). In this design it copies them from the result's materializationState.sources — the per-result record §7 introduced — and never consults value structs at flush. Same rows on disk; an honest in-memory source for them.

The one real encode delta: record both forms when both exist. Today's encoder picks one form — snapshot if the value has one, else lazy (core/directory.go:225: the snapshot branch returns early). R1 wants "both when possible": a value that has a snapshot and a lazy form should persist both, so losing the snapshot later still leaves a path. That is the whole change — a small encode adjustment, no new schema.

And nothing needs a "retainability" concept. Whether a result can be recomputed falls out of what its payload factually is: a value with no in-engine lazy form simply encodes without one. The canonical example: host.directory(…) results — the "recipe" is an upload from the connected client's machine, so there is no lazy struct to persist; and since its call identity is deliberately never shared (RequestedCacheInput("noCache"), core/schema/host.go:47 — per-client by default, per-call under noCache), no later call ever derives its key anyway — it matters only as an input referenced by downstream lazy forms, and through its content class (R16's clarification). No marking, no taxonomy: viability at restore is just "does the payload have something usable" — D2's job.

One encoding serves local persistence now and bundles later (R9): a bundle is this same per-result encoding minus the engine-local rows (no integer keys, no refKeys — R3/R4), which the next decision makes possible.

PROPOSED AMENDMENT A5 — "persist both forms" must capture the recipe at publication, because realization destroys it · awaiting owner review · rewritten for clarity (round 12)

What D1 promised. When a value has both a snapshot and a lazy recipe, persist both (R1's "both when possible") — so if the snapshot is lost later, the recipe path still exists. D1 called that "a small encode adjustment" at flush time. It cannot be done at flush time at all, and here is why.

The engine deliberately throws the recipe away the moment it finishes running. Two places, both on the success path:

  1. publish — the value is created; the recipe is present. This is the only moment both are guaranteed to exist.
  2. first use / realize — the recipe runs; the snapshot now exists; the recipe object is destroyed (the code above).
  3. shutdown flush — only the snapshot is left in memory. "Persist both" has nothing left to persist.

(The decoders hold the mirror-image assumption — a persisted payload is either snapshot-form or lazy-form, e.g. the switch at core/file.go:284/291 — so an envelope carrying both is also a small format change on the read side.)

Proposed change. The live recipe object is still destroyed at realization exactly as today (release behavior unchanged); the stashed JSON survives on the sharedResult; flush writes snapshot + stashed recipe; decoders learn to accept an envelope carrying both (covered by the schema_version bump this take already requires). This is also the concrete answer to the reviewer's "shrink materializationState to an augmentation" suggestion: the sharedResult's record is the one object whose lifetime covers both sides — it outlives the recipe (destroyed at realization) and any particular value struct (cloned and discarded freely). That lifetime is why the consolidation exists.

DECISION — R16 BECOMES DESIGN
Lookup keys are process-local. Persisted identity is digests, full stop.
  • On disk, authoritative: self_digest per term, the term→input-class structure (term_inputs), and each class's digest set (eq_class_digests). Integer class/term IDs remain in the file — but as ordinary foreign keys stitching rows together, never as identity.
  • At boot — main's existing recompute, made authoritative: import already recomputes every term key (calcEgraphTermDigest re-derived per term, cache_persistence_import.go:272) and then validates it against the stored column, where any mismatch is an import failure — a full store wipe today (import.go:274). This design keeps the recompute and deletes the comparison: the persisted term_digest column is removed — a column nothing authoritative reads is cruft — which also deletes that wipe trigger outright. In-memory class identity derives from eq_class_digests (the digest→class map); integer IDs — file or memory — are joins, never identity. schema_version bumps; existing stores wipe on upgrade, the sanctioned cost of a format change.
  • While serving — unchanged from main: a new value's content digest is associated with its class synchronously at publication, under the cache's lock (teachResultIdentityLocked). This design changes nothing there.
  • The property test (R16 is "cheaply testable"): boot the same store twice, derive all keys both times, require identical hit behavior for identical calls — a determinism unit test, plus the restart suite as the integration check. Standing caveat (owner-ruled): if this test ever trips on what looks like legitimate nondeterminism, stop and escalate for discussion — assess real-defect vs benign source and decide deliberately; do not contort the design to satisfy the test.
  • Bundle compatibility for free: since nothing authoritative depends on file-local integers, a future bundle carries digests and frames only, and import treats it exactly like a second digest source — no parallel identity scheme (R9).

D2 — Restore: per-result vetting, drop-with-dependents

The mechanics worth pinning: vetting runs dependencies-first (one topological pass over result_deps), so "lazy form viable" can require "the results it references were kept" without iteration; a dropped result drops its dependents in the same pass (reverse edges); import_failure stops being a store-wipe reason — a row that fails to decode is just a non-viable result. Two more rules keep salvage deliberately dumb (owner-ruled: corruption is dropped or wiped, never cleverly repaired — convoluted corrupt-data handling is where bugs live): a dependency cycle is treated as corruption of every result in it — the strongly-connected component drops together, with its dependents (cycles cannot legitimately occur, so salvage never guesses); and snapshot owner leases are attached only after vetting, only for kept results, with stale lease IDs from previously-dropped results deleted in the same pass, so a dropped row can never pin content. What restore does not do: touch content, execute recipes, or decode envelopes (beyond structural validation) — boot never touches content (R14, §7's worked example step 1).

Beyond vetting: runtime source failure, and the fallback floor

Vetting is boot-time honesty about local facts. It cannot promise the future: a snapshot can be pruned after boot, and in the remote future a content pull can fail at use time (the metadata said available; the transfer returns 500s). Two rules complete R2 at runtime:

  1. Failures fall through, and propagate up dependency chains naturally. A source that fails at use time falls through to the result's next source (§7, Fig. 4) — and a lazy form fails if a result it references cannot itself be materialized, so a failure deep in a chain surfaces as source failure at the result being served.
  2. The floor: demote the hit to a miss. When the result being served exhausts its sources, the caller's own live call executes, with the caller's own live inputs. Every serving flows from a live session call, so this floor always exists; the fresh execution publishes and re-teaches equivalence, healing the store as a side effect.

The upload case, end to end — the scenario that motivated this panel:

  1. WARM SESSION
    The caller's session uploads its directory — host.directory, the caller's client is attached; the content digest is computed at upload (mirror.Snapshot → ref, contentDgst, host.go:314) — and calls withExec on it. The lookup content-matches the persisted withExec → a hit.
  2. SERVING FAILS FORWARD
    Serving needs content: the withExec's snapshot source fails (pruned locally, or the remote pull 500s). Fall through to its lazy form — the exec — which needs its input directory materialized.
  3. THE INPUT IS AN UPLOAD — NOTHING TO REPLAY, STRUCTURALLY
    The persisted input row has a snapshot source and nothing else: there is no lazy struct on disk that could ever "re-run the upload" (host.go:320 builds the value as {Dir, Snapshot} — no Lazy). Its snapshot is gone too → the input cannot be materialized → the withExec's lazy-form source fails.
  4. THE FLOOR
    Sources exhausted → demote to miss: the caller's withExec executes live against the caller's own upload (already materialized in their session at step 1). Correct result, no stranded hit, no replayed upload — and the new execution publishes, healing the store.

Ownership boundary: the precise mechanics — waiter semantics when several sessions hold the same failing hit, the error taxonomy, exactly what re-teaches — belong to §9 (warm lookup & on-demand materialization), and this scenario is pre-registered as §9's worked example. What is settled here: the fallback floor exists, and the dangerous artifact — a replayable upload recipe — structurally does not.

D3 — Flush: unchanged cadence, honest reads

Flush stays a clean-shutdown snapshot (R14's read-mostly serving window doesn't change), with two differences: it builds each result's rows from materializationState (D1), and it writes the R10 self-check alongside the markers — per-boot counts {results_total, results_executed_this_boot, results_imported} into meta and the debug snapshot. The R10 assertion ("import → re-export adds zero rows") becomes a test that boots a store, flushes it untouched, and diffs the counts — measured, not assumed (§7's growth model).

9. Warm lookup and on-demand materialization (ratified round 9)

The idea in three sentences. A warm call finds its result through the same two lookups main runs today — exact digests first, then the structural e-graph match — filtered by the session-resource gate, taking the first eligible candidate as the winner. Serving the winner means realizing content on demand through the existing lazy-evaluation machinery, now walking §7's source list with fall-through instead of assuming the snapshot is there. And when every source is exhausted, the hit demotes to a miss: the caller's own live call executes — the floor that keeps R2 honest under runtime failure.

Names here are the codebase's own: the two lookup stages are lookupMatchForDigestsLocked (recipe digest, then extra digests including content) and lookupMatchForCallLocked (the structural term match) — cache_egraph.go:658/683; eligibility is sessionSatisfiesResourceRequirementsLocked (:633) and the winner is what selectLookupCandidateForSessionLocked (:646) returns: the first eligible candidate in deterministic set order. Realize = §7's materialization; demote = fall back to executing the caller's live call.

9.1  Current state on main

  • Lookup is already two-stage and already winner-based. Exact digests first — recipe digest, then extra digests including the content digest (lookupMatchForDigestsLocked, cache_egraph.go:658) — then the structural term match over input equivalence classes (lookupMatchForCallLocked, :683). Candidates are filtered by the session-resource subset gate (:633 — a result requiring secret/socket handles is invisible to sessions not holding them, R7) and the first eligible one wins (:646).
  • Serving a restored result is deferred decode + lazy evaluation (§7 Fact 2, §8 D1): the envelope decodes on first use; lazy forms re-run through shared.lazyEval's singleflight — one runner, N waiters, last-waiter-cancels, retry-on-failure.
  • What main does not have: the decode path assumes snapshot refs are valid — a ref that died after boot is a hard error at use time (a stranded hit, R2's named enemy); there is no source fall-through, no demote floor, and no reuse counters to prove any of this behavior in tests.

9.2  Design

D1 — Lookup and the winner: no changes

This design adds nothing to the lookup path. Restored sharedResults enter the same digest and term indexes at boot (§8 D2, with R16's recomputed keys) and compete as ordinary candidates. The session-resource gate already implements R7 at exactly the right place — candidate eligibility — and it is transitive and persisted (§8; cache.go:254–277, import.go:426/682). Winner selection stays "first eligible in deterministic order": introducing a preference (say, realized-before-unrealized) is a real optimization with a real cost in policy surface, and nothing in the requirements demands it — recorded as a future option, not built (L5).

D2 — Realization: the lazy runner walks the sources

The existing lazy singleflight is kept exactly as-is — one runner, N waiters, last-waiter-cancels, retry-on-failure. What changes is the body: it becomes the materialization entry that owns the source walk — unifying what today is the pair "ensurePersistedHitValueLoaded (decode, currently under its own persistDecodeMu singleflight) → lazy evaluation" behind one entry that walks the winner's source list (§7, Fig. 4), called from the hit path and from Evaluate alike:

// the lazy runner's body, sketched — dagql/cache.go

func (c *Cache) realize(shared *sharedResult) error {
	for _, src := range shared.materialization.sources {
		if !src.viable() {                        // marked non-viable earlier? skip
			continue
		}
		err := src.realizeInto(shared)            // snapshot: verify refs + lease + decode
		                                          // lazy form: materialize referenced inputs
		                                          //   (recursively, demand-bounded — R12),
		                                          //   then re-run the persisted lazy struct
		if err == nil {
			shared.materialization.realized = true
			return nil                            // serve; lazy-arrival write point (§7)
		}
		if isPermanent(err) {                     // e.g. refKey gone from the worker store
			src.markNonViable(err)                // don't retry a dead source forever
		}
		// fall through to the next source (R2)
	}
	return errSourcesExhausted                    // → the caller demotes (D3)
}

Two rules, stated once:

  • Propagation: a lazy form fails if a result it references cannot itself be materialized — so a failure deep in a dependency chain surfaces as source failure at the result being served, with no per-type special cases (§8's "beyond vetting" panel).
  • Marking: permanent failures (a refKey missing from the worker store) mark that source non-viable on the sharedResult so retries don't spin on a dead source; transient failures (cancellation, resource pressure) don't mark anything — the existing retry-on-failure semantics mean the next waiter's attempt walks the list again.

D3 — The floor: demote to a miss

When realize exhausts the sources, every waiter receives the terminal sources-exhausted outcome, and each caller then executes its own live call — the call it was already making, with its own live inputs, under its own session (R6 principal semantics come free: the executor is the caller). The fresh execution publishes normally, and publication's content-digest teaching (TeachContentDigest, cache_egraph.go:988 — synchronous, under the lock) re-unifies the new result with the old equivalence class: the store heals as a side effect, and the next caller hits. The floor always exists because every serving flows from a live session call; it is per-caller (two sessions demoting produce their own executions, deduplicated by the ordinary in-flight singleflight on identical calls); and it is final — there is nothing below it, which is what makes R2's promise honest rather than hopeful.

And exhaustion is terminal for the result, not just for the attempt: when the walk exhausts with every failure permanent, the result is dropped, with its dependents — the same rule boot vetting applies (§8 D2): deindexed from digest and term candidacy and released, under the cache's ordinary locking. One rule, two moments: "zero viable sources ⇒ the result is dropped," at boot or at runtime. The next lookup finds only whatever the demoted callers freshly published — which is exactly what makes P-9.2's heal assertion provable. Transient exhaustion (cancellation, resource pressure) drops nothing: the next waiter retries the walk.

D4 — Cross-client ID loads: loader-flavored by design

A loaded ID is a capability in the access-control sense — possessing it grants access to the result it names: the fast lookup honors it exactly (the ID's own digest + its extra digests, server.go:1364), and the structural lookup preserves the minting client's implicit inputs as literals (server.go:1528). But if the named result is truly gone and execution is required, the final fallback runs through the normal resolve path, where implicit inputs are re-resolved from the executing context (objects.go:545; PerClientInput.Resolver reads the live client, cache_inputs.go:14) — so a different client loading the ID gets a loader-flavored re-evaluation: their client identity, their filesystem, their secrets. For the same client this is identical semantics; across clients it is the only sound option and matches the trust model (handing someone an ID grants access to the result, not to your machine). Sanctioned explicitly here so it is never "fixed."

D5 — Observability for this section: a scoped un-shelving

This section's gate needs to prove reuse and demotion, which is exactly the consumer the shelved observability milestone was waiting for. Off the shelf comes the minimal slice (per §5's rule — specified here, with its consumer): the outcome vocabulary and per-field counters — hit_live, hit_restored, miss_first, plus this section's additions served_from{snapshot|lazy_form} and demoted_to_miss — classified exactly once at the lookup/serving terminal paths; one debug-level structured line per event (the diagnosis path); the shutdown stats file next to the persisted DB (the test-assertion vehicle — files, not log streams) plus the same data in the debug snapshot and a one-line session summary. Bounded by rule: every counter and surface here is consumed by a named gate case (T1, T2, §8's T6); anything without a consuming test does not land, and additions require a new consumer first. Still shelved: strict modes, per-session tallies, the forensics ring — no consumer yet.

9.3  Worked example — the upload scenario, verified against the code

§8 pre-registered this scenario; it has since been traced through the actual machinery (review round 8). Client B loads an ID minted by client A: a withExec whose input was A's host.directory upload. The remote/pruned worst case, end to end:

  1. LOOKUP HONORS THE CAPABILITY
    The load's fast lookup uses the ID's own digest plus its extra digests — including the content digest — so it hits A's exact row when present, or any content-equivalent row (server.go:1364). On fast-miss, the structural lookup preserves A's per-client implicit input as a literal from the ID (server.go:1528) and can still match through the e-graph.
  2. B'S OWN SESSION MADE THE MATCH POSSIBLE
    Where the hit came via content equivalence: B's session already uploaded its own directory — B's client is attached — and the upload's content digest was taught at publication, synchronously, under the lock (WithContentDigest → TeachContentDigest → teachResultIdentityLocked, host.go:313/332, cache_egraph.go:988). Same bytes ⇒ B's class unified with the imported class ⇒ the withExec term matched. The union-find doing precisely its job.
  3. SERVING FAILS FORWARD
    The withExec winner needs content: the snapshot source fails (pruned locally, or the remote pull 500s). The runner falls through to the lazy form — the exec — which needs its input directory materialized (D2).
  4. NOTHING TO REPLAY, STRUCTURALLY
    A's upload row has a snapshot source and nothing else — host.directory builds its value with no Lazy at all (host.go:320), so no lazy struct was ever persisted. Its snapshot is gone too ⇒ the input cannot materialize ⇒ the withExec's lazy-form source fails by propagation (D2). And had anything ever tried to execute an upload call, the per-client input re-resolves to the executing client and SpecificClientAttachableConn (host.go:280) demands that client be attached — a clean error, never a fabricated replay.
  5. THE FLOOR, LANDING ON B'S OWN INPUT
    Sources exhausted ⇒ demote (D3): B's withExec executes live against B's own already-materialized upload from step 2. Correct result, no stranded hit, nothing replayed; the execution publishes, re-teaches, and heals the store — demoted_to_miss ticks once (D5), which is how the gate proves this path rather than assuming it.

The same round audited the neighboring client-resource family — filesync mirrors (keyed by stable client ID), client secrets and SSH sockets (salted handles taught as content digests; gated by the transitive session-resource requirements at candidate eligibility), host services (caller-module scoped) — and found the same shape everywhere: per-client identity where execution needs the client, content/handle equivalence where sharing is safe, session-resource gating where access must be proven. Known deferred item, unchanged: the per-engine secret salt partitions handles across engines (bites the remote take, not restart).

Why shelved (owner ruling, round 3): no consumer exists yet, and designing a concurrency primitive against hypothetical consumers is how policy knobs (sticky/retryable) appear before anything needs them. The draft was also, honestly, partly carry-over reflex from the previous take's equivalent. If a design section develops a real need, the mechanism gets designed there, with its consumer — and whether the existing hand-rolled protocols then migrate onto it is an explicit boilerplate-vs-abstraction decision (lesson L5) made at that point. Load-bearing open question recorded in §6: on-demand materialization of restored results may simply be the existing lazy-evaluation path — in which case no new wait/claim machinery ever exists. This shelf entry must not bias that question.

What the draft contained, kept as grounding for that future decision:

  • The pattern it targeted ("many callers wait on one deferred piece of work"), hand-rolled today in three places on main, each slightly different: ongoingCall's waitCh/waiters (dagql/cache.go:1745–1757); the lazy-evaluation protocol (dagql/cache.go:2802–3032) with last-waiter cleanup and ordering enforced by comment (":2977"); the restored-result dependency-attachment barrier (dagql/cache_persistence_import.go:563–576).
  • The primitive it proposed: an error-only "stage" (payloads live on the owning object, written before completion; the channel close provides the happens-before edge) with explicit waiter accounting, last-waiter cancellation with cause, caller-detached execution context, and two completion policies (freeze-first-outcome for publication; retry-on-failure for lazy evaluation and materialization). Its correctness pitch: no shared error observed outside the synchronization that produced it, and no ordering that lives in a comment.
  • The "claim" idea — N concurrent sessions racing to use a restored result whose content isn't materialized: one wins and materializes, others wait, failure releases so the next waiter retries the next source (R2's fall-through). This is the hypothetical consumer; it only becomes real if the warm-lookup design needs a mechanism distinct from lazy evaluation.
