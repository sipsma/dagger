1. What we're building
A CI run executes on some engine. The results it produces — cache metadata, and content when it's worth moving —
become available to every other engine in the same Dagger Cloud organization. A later run on a fresh engine adopts
equivalent results as ordinary cache hits and materializes their content only on demand: downloading it when
available, re-executing when not. The payoff is warm CI on cold machines.
The division of labor is deliberate and load-bearing. The open-source engine is the executor and the local cache: it
runs builds, reports the state of its cache and what it's executing, and performs transfers it's asked to perform.
The intelligence lives in the proprietary Cloud service (the dagger.io repo): deciding what's worth collecting from
which engine, what an upcoming job will probably need and should be prepositioned, what to prune from the pool — and,
ultimately, when remote caching would not help and should be skipped. Remote caching is a monetizable
product capability, and that shapes the architecture on purpose.
The lifecycle is loose rather than checkpointed. Instead of "import at boot, export at end of job," engines and the
service share a communication channel, and the service can act at any point: "I'm pretty sure you have this result —
upload it when you get a chance," or "here's metadata you'll probably want — import it." The old rigid lifecycle
remains expressible as a special case of this; the looseness is the point, because it makes many more systems
expressible without (we believe) many more corner cases than already exist at baseline.
The immediate next work is not the service. It is the foundational engine work: making the engine cache able
to hold results computed elsewhere and serve them as ordinary hits, pulling snapshot content only when a build
actually needs it. That work is designed from scratch, blocks on no cloud-side work, and merges incrementally
(G25). Its design, decisions, and proposed order live in the
Engine Foundations doc. The working direction there: a result learned from
another engine enters the cache as a lazy result — metadata filled, each snapshot pulled on demand,
falling back to running the result's own recipe when a pull fails — and evaluation becomes fine-grained enough that
reading a container's metadata never pulls its layers. A large share of that is improvement to the engine's existing
laziness, valuable with no remote cache at all, and it lands first.
2. Requirements and principles
Product boundary
G1settled
The engine executes; the service thinks. The Cloud service owns the intelligent decisions: what is worth
collecting, what an upcoming CI job will probably need, when to preposition it, what to prune. The engine tells the
service about the general state of its cache and what it's executing, and carries out transfers. "Dumb engine" is
not literal or extreme: the engine of course keeps whatever durable local bookkeeping it needs and participates in
ordinary local decisions (what's exportable, source viability, fallback, dependency mechanics). What the engine never
does is the product-level reasoning about value and prediction. There is also no concept of the engine rejecting a
command as a matter of policy — "I can't do that" is simply a normal protocol outcome, and the service shouldn't be
issuing nonsensical commands in the first place. The exact decision split beyond this is worked out per real
behavior, expected to be mostly obvious — but it must be decided explicitly each time, never silently assumed.
G2settled
Everything remote is proprietary; the OSS engine keeps what it has. The service, its storage backends, and the
integration/end-to-end tests for remote caching all live in the closed-source dagger.io repo (cross-repo test
mechanics are a detail for later; plenty of options). The OSS repo keeps the current level of local cache
persistence — persist on shutdown, restore on boot — plus whatever engine-side remote-cache mechanics this effort
adds. Engine-side unit tests in OSS are fine; fake remote-cache servers or test services in OSS are not.
G3settled
The client protocol is ordinary, open code. No obfuscation, no binary blobs, no secrecy theater. Someone could
read the engine-side protocol and build a compatible server; that's fine and not worth a single step to prevent. The
proprietary value is the service's analysis and behavior, not the wire format.
G4settled
Supported environment: CI run by Dagger's proprietary backend. That is it. Not local developer engines, not
self-hosted engines, not users' own infrastructure, not offline engines. This is a major simplifying constraint —
lean on it.
G5settled
The sharing scope is the Cloud org, and the engine doesn't model it. Cache is shared within an org; no
cross-org sharing, no finer-than-org scopes for now (tiers may come much later). Because engines only run under the
managed CI backend (G4), the endpoint already knows which engine and which org it's talking to and derives the scope
itself. The engine just talks to the endpoint; it has no remote-cache reason to understand organizations.
Trust
G6settled
Everything inside the scope is trusted. There is no such thing as hostile input. Engines are assumed to work
correctly; users are assumed to operate as desired; service metadata, manifests, and transfer inputs are trusted.
No cache poisoning concept, no malicious-engine modeling, no defensive verification burden anywhere in the system.
In Erik's words: hostile input "is a useless notion which plants a seed of outrageous complexity solving problems
that do not exist." Any mechanism whose justification is distrust inside the scope is over-engineering by
definition. Production hardening may revisit some of this much later; it is not a design input now.
G7settled (stopgap)
Secrets: today's rules, plus exactly one stopgap. Secret-, socket-, and host-state-dependent results already
cross client and session boundaries today — engines serve multiple clients on different machines, and cache identity
already accounts for these resources. Sharing across engines is the same problem and is treated as already solved;
no new cross-engine rules are justified. (Erik holds this confidently and invites concrete counter-evidence.) The one
sanctioned piece of work: make the engine's secret salt overridable, and run every engine in an org with the same
salt — an explicitly temporary mitigation so secret-identity partitioning doesn't block cache work. The secret system
itself is otherwise untouched; the real design is revisited after the core work settles.
Status: the salt override landed on main (#13925).
The cache model
G8settled
Interchangeability is absolute, and there is one cache model.
"If two things are considered the same by the cache, they are interchangeable. Full stop, end of story."
Remote and local hits use the same equivalence contract. "Cross-engine reuse" is not a category distinct from the
cross-session reuse the cache does today, and no remote-only preference, ordering, gating, or consistency rule may
exist. If we ever dislike an interchangeability outcome, that means changing the cache itself — a deliberate, named
act — never a remote-cache specialization layered on top. This rule exists because past efforts demonstrably
over-thought and over-complicated exactly this point. Across engines the rule rests on G6 and G7: a result carrying
the same digests on another engine in the org is interchangeable here, and the engine's own e-graph derives that
equivalence from the digests exactly as it does for local results — the service never asserts equivalence
directly.
G9settled (direction)
Ambient inputs get fixed in the cache key, at the source. If engine-local state shapes a result — platform,
configuration, anything read off the engine rather than the call — its concrete value belongs in the normal cache
identity, so equivalent work matches and different work partitions. Never solve this with remote-only partitioning
rules (that would violate G8). Known concrete lead: the default platform. Erik's recollection is that the
cache key currently mixes in the concept "default" rather than the engine's actual platform, so two engines
with different native platforms could derive equal identities for different results. The code shape supports this:
container(platform:...) is optional and, when omitted, resolves parent.Platform() at
execution time, after call identity is formed (core/schema/container.go:1004–1012). Before fixing:
audit for compensating identity-mixing elsewhere, then audit for similar implicit ambient inputs. The fix is always
the same — make the value explicit in the key.
Status: the default-platform fix landed on main (#13911). An audit for other implicit ambient inputs was run
by another agent: most of its findings were noise, and the one real finding was unrelated to this effort. Erik does
not trust that audit, so the question stays open: everyone working in the cache keeps an eye out for
identity that depends on engine-local state rather than on the call, and raises it when seen.
G10settled (direction)
Timing, not transactions. The cache is not a database. There is no global ordering, no preferred history, no
consistency ceremony — everything that happens in the engine cache is timing-based. When equivalent work races,
whoever wins, wins, and the rest is tossed. The only obligations: racing must not produce errors, must not leave
broken internal state, and interchangeability (G8) is honored.
G11settled semantics · open mechanism
A miss is never an error, and the fall-through is fixed in meaning. Use the local snapshot if present; else,
if content is known to be downloadable, try to download and use it — tolerating failure, because network errors are
real; else it's a cache miss, which means run the thing — and running requires knowing the recipe. That
semantic contract is settled. The mechanism now has a working direction (the
Engine Foundations doc): the fall-through lives inside the result's own
deferred work — evaluating a part of a learned result means pull that part's content, else run the result's recipe
— so a failed pull is never an error, just the next step. What remains open inside that direction is recorded in
OQ7 and OQ8. The old efforts' source models are evidence, not defaults.
G12settled
Remote caching is invisible to pipeline correctness. Service outages, stale availability, missing results,
failed downloads, unusable imports — all degrade to normal execution and never change a pipeline's outcome. Erik puts
this at ~99% strict: a reservation exists only for some unknown case where transparency would cost a massive universe
of complexity. The fallback machinery itself must stay proportionate — "it's not conceptually that crazy to have a
fallback; let's make sure the system isn't either."
G13settled
Session end does not mean gone, and the local cache lifecycle stays fundamentally as-is. Today, persistable
results and their retained dependencies commonly survive the session that created them; that behavior is not
changing in any deep way. Local pruning also stays local — driven by disk size and the engine's own settings — so a
result can disappear before the service asks for it. That is a normal race to tolerate (G17), not a problem to
prevent. Whether engines ever proactively resync deletion state to the service is open (OQ11).
G14settled, careful zone
Every layer is a cache; nothing is ever the only copy of something the user can't regenerate. Engine store,
service metadata, blob storage: all prunable, all losable, all rebuildable from future runs. Builds never fail
because of a cache miss or an ephemeral network error while fetching cache. The careful part: preciousness is
decided per case, explicitly, because both extremes are real failure modes — protecting things that need no
protection is over-engineering, and demanding a fallback for every conceivable error is over-engineering too. When
one of these details surfaces, it gets written down and ruled on (G26, OQ15), not absorbed
silently in either direction.
G15settled
Mutable state does not cross engines. Out of scope for remote caching: mutable cache volumes (crucial shared
understanding), the full mutable filesync mirror of a client's directory, and the mutable git checkout stores.
Genuinely open, not committed: frozen, snapshotted forms of client directories and git sources
(OQ10). More items in this category will surface; each gets an explicit ruling.
Architecture direction
G16preliminary
A loose lifecycle over a standing channel — and we build the channel. The service can act at any point:
request uploads of results it believes an engine has, or offer metadata worth importing. No moment is special;
import-at-boot / export-at-end survives only as one expressible policy among many. The channel is purpose-built by
this effort (the first bidirectional service⇄engine channel we've needed); its transport form — persistent
bidirectional connection, long-polling, plain requests, or a hybrid — is genuinely open with real trade-offs around
stickiness and statefulness (OQ3). Adjacent trajectory to design with: engine telemetry is headed
toward direct engine→cloud export (currently reverted on main; see #13811), which is how the service's awareness of
engine activity keeps growing. The foundational engine batch blocks on none of this.
G17preliminary
Commands are hints; coordination is best-effort; availability leases are out. Distributed knowledge will be
stale: the service may ask for something an engine has pruned, and "I no longer have that" is an expected, accepted
answer — never grounds for reserving results on engines, forcing recomputation, or durable engine-side command
queues. Erik on distributed leases: "hell no." The cloud-forces-rerun pattern is both horrifically complicated and
technically impossible; it does not exist here.
G18preliminary
Foundations assume live import. Imported metadata can arrive while the engine is serving — the old
startup-only-import assumption is not carried forward, and new foundations must not bake it in. How imports become
visible (one by one, batched, or at coarse safe application points under the engine's own locking) is open: start
with whatever is simplest while still effective, with a mild lean toward coarse safe points
(OQ5). Fine-grained continuous merging stays on the table as a later generalization — nothing is
built for it now, and nothing should foreclose it cheaply.
G19settled
No synchronous miss-path RPC. A local cache miss never blocks on a network question. Imports are pushed or
prefetched metadata; content moves lazily; misses just execute.
G20preliminary
The service maintains a queryable metadata pool. Queryable enough to make upload and serve decisions
server-side — that much is agreed. Everything past that sentence is open: the data model, merge and idempotence
semantics, and whether any engine code runs server-side (OQ12).
G21preliminary
Uploads: the engine is told what to upload; bytes move via presigned URLs. The working sketch: the engine
hits a service API that responds with an HTTP redirect to a presigned upload URL — which as a side effect tells the
service the engine at least attempted the upload. The sketch could change in subtle or fundamental ways;
acknowledgement, retry, and lifecycle semantics are open (OQ4). The load-bearing part is only the
decision split: the service decides what's worth uploading, the engine executes.
Development posture
G22settled
Zero compatibility work of any kind until production nears. We are developing; there is no such thing as
compatibility yet. Hard protocol cuts; run known-compatible engines and services together; happily invalidate entire
cache pools whenever a format change makes that simplest — until Erik explicitly says otherwise. No version
migration, no persisted-format stability effort, no backwards compatibility with anything — especially not with code
that never shipped anywhere. Named anti-pattern: an agent preserving "backwards compatibility" with its own
uncommitted change from five minutes ago.
G23settled
This is a fresh rewrite; the past is reference, not input. Nothing from the earlier efforts — designs,
lessons, code — carries default authority. The bar for importing a learning: the old work handled something we
didn't, and it actually matters. Both conditions, verified against current reality — then we learned
something valuable. Otherwise, no copying, in any form. Don't run into new designs carrying old assumptions.
G24settled
No code is off-limits — and no invented problems. The e-graph, the ownership model, the laziness system,
any cache internals: all changeable when a real requirement demands it; nothing is frozen, and no agent may ever
declare an area untouchable. The theoretical scope is whatever the effort needs; what actually changes is decided
intentionally, per slice. Equally, never manufacture a problem as an excuse to change code, and never change
behavior unrelated to the effort just because the machinery to change it is nearby. Ruled example: a persisted
value that fails to decode keeps returning an error today, and stays that way — a decode failure means an engine bug
or a hardware failure, which we do not design for.
G25preliminary
Foundations first, merged incrementally, with the end-to-end path plotted — not built — ahead. The bias is
toward getting well-understood foundational slices and independent fixes merged sooner rather than later. Designing
somewhat ahead of the foundations is necessary to know they're the right foundations — and can be overdone;
the balance is worked out in practice. What the foundational batch must produce: an engine cache that fundamentally
supports the model above — foreign results adopted as metadata, content materialized on demand from multiple
possible sources, live import, all under G8's single cache model. Its precise boundary is
OQ14.
Working process
G26preliminary seed
Assumptions get written down; unconfirmed corner cases get denied; the default is stop-and-ask. Three parts,
kept deliberately small: (1) load-bearing assumptions are recorded — a flat ledger, short per-slice lists, or both;
the format is unresolved and must stay lightweight, never a registry bureaucracy. Asking Erik happens in normal
conversation, not through forms. (2) Default-deny: complexity that exists to handle a case Erik hasn't confirmed
real is deletable on sight, no debate owed. (3) When a newly discovered case would materially widen the design,
agents stop and ask; the bias stays toward asking until Erik grants more autonomy in a specific area.
Over-engineering this process itself is a named failure mode.
G27settled
Communication contract. Plain, direct technical English; claims tied to evidence; push back when something
doesn't add up — no yes-manning in either direction. When raising a concern inherited from the old efforts, present
it as a concrete example in current code, never as an old effort's label or abstraction (old jargon has already made
real concerns unintelligible). Human-facing documents are readable HTML, never plain markdown. And there is never a
rush: depth over speed, always.
G28settled
Evidence is slice-specific. There is no universal gate checklist. For every slice of work, ask what proof its
actual risks and invariants require, and choose tests and review gates from that — a question to re-ask each time,
not answer once.
G29open, evolving
Erik's review altitude is a constraint the process must fit. He can skim everything or read every line of
nothing; the target is the middle — the ability to zoom into any detail without living at the microscopic level. His
deep familiarity with the codebase (a ~95%-accurate smell for something being off) is an asset the process should
feed, not bypass. The largest reliably-reviewable unit is not knowable in advance; it gets discovered by doing, and
the process (including Tailcall itself, which evolves alongside) adapts at each real failure.
G30settled
The TLA+ model is part of the definition of done. The cache's concurrency kernel is modeled in
dagql/tla and checked in CI; the model must stay an accurate reflection of the code. For deep changes
the order is: extend the model first to see whether the design holds, write the code to match, then reconcile both
until they agree and the model asserts the invariants and properties we care about. A PR that touches modeled
behavior is production-ready only when model and code are aligned and every asserted property is green; a red
property is a blocker, not a note. No cheating: a green check over states the model cannot reach is worthless, so
new machinery enters with a re-break run (weaken the fix, watch the invariant trip) and a reachability probe.
Baseline areas the effort touches that the model does not yet cover get modeled as part of the work. The model
checks lifecycle and concurrency; tests keep carrying content correctness, performance, and type coverage — it is a
significant tool, not the only one.
G31settled
The recipe-replay system is not an input to this effort. The engine recently gained a system for replaying
recipe-form IDs that are persisted outside the engine, with a "not replayable" marking on certain calls. That was
built by a different effort, for a different use case, with different goals. It is not what remote
caching does, its rules do not apply here, and nothing in this effort may depend on it or be shaped by it unless it
is proven, concretely, to be relevant — the strong expectation is that it is not. Our recipes are the result call
frames and lazy recipes the cache already stores and persists; sharing those between engines is the same kind of
thing persistence already does across restarts. Agents are expected to confuse the two; this item exists so they
do not.
G32settled
Decisions are working positions, never commandments. Every decision recorded in these documents was made
with the information available at the time and can be questioned by anyone at any time. Nothing is 100% set in
stone. A later agent that finds a recorded decision straining against reality must raise it, not contort the
system to preserve it. The record exists so decisions are visible and deliberate, not so they are permanent.
3. Explicit non-goals
Collected here for fast reference; each traces to a principle above. These are valuable precisely because past
efforts sank time into some of them.
Environments: local developer engines · self-hosted or arbitrary user infrastructure · offline engines (G4).
Distribution: cross-org or public/global sharing · scopes finer than an org · multi-region replication (G5).
Adversaries: cache poisoning · malicious engines · hostile input of any kind · defensive verification (G6).
Compatibility: old engine or service versions · protocol version skew · preserving pools across format changes · migration paths (G22).
Mutable state crossing engines: mutable cache volumes · full client filesync mirrors · mutable git checkout stores (G15).
Machinery: distributed availability leases · forced recomputation · database-style ordering or consistency · durable command queues (G17, G10) · synchronous miss-path lookups (G19) · protocol obfuscation (G3) · engine command-rejection policy (G1) · remote-only equivalence or partitioning rules (G8, G9).
Other efforts' machinery: the recipe-replay / "not replayable" system (G31) · behavior changes unrelated to the effort, such as reinterpreting persisted-value decode failures (G24).
Eager content movement: pulling a result's layers because it was learned or hit, or pulling its dependencies' layers recursively — both are baseline requirements violated, not optimizations missed (Engine Foundations doc, F11 and F12).
4. Open questions
Deliberately unanswered. Keeping these open is preferable to importing answers from the old attempts; none may be
resolved silently by implementation. Roughly ordered from product-level to mechanism-level.
OQ1First proving workload. Dagger's own org CI (real value, but a hard first target — testing Dagger requires frequent intentional cache invalidation; its scale is substantial and must eventually work), another org's CI, Tailcall's CI (heavy Dagger use, much simpler), or a smaller constructed scenario; possibly several orgs. Don't turn "fresh engine reuses another engine's result" into a milestone requirement yet.
OQ2How the service learns and names engine cache state. Is telemetry the inventory (service builds its picture from enriched spans), or a trigger with a dedicated authoritative query behind it? What identity does an upload request use? What state do engines emit continuously? Crucial and deliberately deferred until the overall shape settles; avoid stringent machinery — choose what proves straightforward and effective.
OQ3Channel transport. Persistent bidirectional connection (stickiness/statefulness costs), long-polling, separate requests, or a hybrid. "A million options"; decided later.
OQ4Upload command lifecycle. Acknowledgement and retry semantics; what the service learns from the redirect hit vs. object-store completion; engines disappearing mid-command; ephemeral CI engines vs. deferred uploads (linger and drain? overlap the run? something else); which lifecycle transitions get recorded.
OQ5Import application mechanics. Given live import (G18): visibility per-result, batched, or at short safe application points; what "simplest while still effective" concretely is; what evidence would justify moving to fine-grained continuous merge. Working lean (Engine Foundations doc, D9): one result at a time, dependencies before dependents, each row one small locked insertion, rows referencing unknown dependencies rejected the way boot import already rejects dangling references.
OQ6Transfer unit and dependency closure. One result, multiple roots, or a job's inventory; whether the engine, the service, or both compute the required closure. Partly shaped by OQ5's lean: whatever the unit, rows arrive dependencies-first.
OQ7What a learned entry carries, and how content is identified. Working direction (Engine Foundations doc): a description carries the result's metadata, call frame and digests, dependency references, a content identity (ordered list of layer identities) for each snapshot the result links, and its recipe. Layer identities are computed at export, never eagerly during builds (the historical performance trap, re-verified against current code: identities do not exist before export). Still open: whether producers keep recipes after evaluation or consumers rebuild them from the call frame (D10), and exactly when chain identities are computed relative to upload (D17).
OQ8Re-execution on the final fall-through arm. Resolved as a working direction (Engine Foundations doc): the fall-through lives inside the learned result's own deferred work. Evaluating a part of a learned result means pull that part's content, else run the result's recipe — the same lazy machinery a locally-persisted result uses after a restart, with a pull step in front. The earlier sub-question ("does every demand sit under a live call?") is moot under this direction, because the fallback never depends on the client's call. Open inside it: recipe availability (D10) and what a result remembers after a failed pull (D14).
OQ9Failure classification. Which source failures are retryable vs. permanently exhausted vs. ordinary-miss grounds. No prior classifier is accepted by default.
OQ10Result-type coverage. Which result types cross engines at all — a high-value whitelist with everything else recomputing, or broader; where frozen client-directory and frozen git-source snapshots land (G15's maybes). "A whole universe of its own to explore." Working answer (Engine Foundations doc, D16): anything the engine can persist today can be shared; plain values are filled at import; snapshot-bearing types (containers, directories, files) get per-part lazy pulls. Results that are not persistable today (containers holding secrets, sockets, or services; the exec that runs a module function) are not shared. The frozen client-directory and git-source questions stay open.
OQ11Pruning and deletion sync. Does the service merely tolerate stale availability (the floor, per G17), or do engines sometimes resynchronize what actually remains locally?
OQ12Service metadata pool internals. Data model; merge and idempotence semantics (re-announcing known state should probably be cheap and harmless, but even that is not yet a requirement); whether engine code is reused server-side.
OQ13Performance objective. No thresholds yet — only "meaningful improvement in supported CI cases." The long-term system decides when remote caching helps and skips it when it wouldn't; what v1 must demonstrate is undecided.
OQ14The foundational batch boundary. Which engine mechanics must land before any end-to-end path exists, and how much of that path must be designed (not built) to prove the foundations have a real consumer. Proposal under review: the staged order in the Engine Foundations doc, which splits the work into baseline laziness improvements (land first, no remote semantics) and remote-specific mechanics (land after). Stays open until Erik approves that order.
OQ15Preciousness rulings. The running, case-by-case record of what may be dropped versus what must survive (feeds G14/G26). Expected to grow continuously; starts empty on purpose.
OQ16Calibration examples of past over-engineering. Erik's memory of specific "case XYZ isn't real" jaw-droppers has faded; concrete, evidence-backed examples from the old branches are needed to jog it and to calibrate the default-deny rule (G26). One instance already confirmed: past over-complication of interchangeability itself (G8). Owner: Fable.
OQ17Institutional memory and consolidation. Where durable truth lives long-term (in-repo docs, this page, agent memory), who owns continuity across the coming fan-out, and who merges future scoping input.
OQ18The old lessons file (L1–L8). The July effort's process lessons await deliberate re-adjudication — an honest hour-plus each, deferred until it earns priority. They carry no authority meanwhile. One known collision to resolve then: L2's standing per-chunk integration gates vs. G28's slice-specific evidence.
OQ19Near-term mergeable fixes. Resolved. The ongoingCall.isPersistable race fix (#13923), the salt override (#13925), and the default-platform identity fix (#13911) merged on main. The lost-cancel on the miss path was not independently reachable in practice and was fixed in passing by the cache-remediation stack (#13936). That stack (#13934, #13936, #13938, #13940) fixes everything the TLA+ model found at baseline; all 25 model configurations are green on it, and the foundational work is based on its head.
